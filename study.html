Inside Royal Match

How a simple match-3 loop supports layered progression, difficulty pacing and LiveOps.

An 11-slide teardown converted into a readable design analysis, preserving the distinction between observed systems and personal estimates.

CORE LOOPDIFFICULTYMETA
Read research document ↗
THE DESIGN QUESTION

The central observation: a familiar match-3 interaction feeds a much larger progression loop through stars, renovation tasks, new areas and timed features.

Core-to-meta loop
01Play match-3→02Earn stars→03Complete area tasks→04Unlock new areas→05Return to gameplay

01 / What I broke down

The original deconstruction examines the core-to-meta loop, levels and progression, bonus and LiveOps features, offer strategy and marketing.

  • The match-3 board creates the repeatable action; stars connect wins to room objectives.
  • Progression rewards are layered: new areas, cards, boosters, event tasks and team participation.
  • LiveOps adds timed return reasons alongside the persistent renovation goal.

02 / Difficulty pacing

My sample 20-level curve uses moves and targets as core difficulty variables. The 1–5 difficulty weights in the original deck are a personal assessment—not official game telemetry.

  • Mix easier levels with harder peaks rather than increasing difficulty continuously.
  • Give boosters a clear role at bottlenecks.
  • Use progression rewards and event beats to offset repeated failure.

03 / Monetization and motivation

The deck reviews IAP offers, timed offer chains and the game's ad/marketing positioning. These are a designer's reading of the experience, not internal product metrics.

  • Currency sinks and valued offers intersect with player progression.
  • Timed events create return incentives and stronger reward anticipation.
  • System layering should remain readable despite multiple live features.
WHAT THIS STUDY SHOWS

Design interpretation: the core loop is easy to grasp, while the meta and events provide long-term reasons to keep playing.

ASO: Competitor & Keyword Research

An approach to competitor discovery, download mix, keyword research and market positioning.

A shorter, navigable version of a 16-slide ASO process deck using fashion and dress-up games as its working example.

RESEARCHPOSITIONINGASO
Read research overview ↗
THE DESIGN QUESTION

The deck treats app-store optimization as a structured research problem: identify comparable titles, inspect where their demand comes from, and map relevant discovery terms.

Research sequence
01Define theme / genre→02Find competitors→03Compare download mix→04Research keywords→05Refine candidate set

01 / Discover comparable games

The research starts with relevant title/theme terms, then expands into broader genre queries. Its working example is Indian wedding, dress-up, fashion and makeup games.

  • Look at daily volume alongside cumulative installs.
  • Cross-check competitors by theme and genre.
  • Keep a shortlist of genuinely comparable player experiences.

02 / Evaluate the data

The document compares paid and organic acquisition ratios, country splits and competitive keywords. Named third-party tools are part of the original research workflow.

  • Separate paid visibility from organic search signals.
  • Consider market-specific differences before applying a global conclusion.
  • Choose screening criteria to fit the research question; internal thresholds are not included in this public overview.

03 / Make the research useful

Tables of competitors and search terms become an actionable positioning brief only after connecting keyword intent to a game's actual promise.

  • Document why a competitor belongs in the shortlist.
  • Show keyword clusters instead of a raw keyword dump.
  • Revalidate download figures before using the research for a new launch.
WHAT THIS STUDY SHOWS

Good ASO research is a repeatable comparison framework, not just a collection of popular keywords.

SOURCE & CONTEXT

The original tables are historical examples. Their download, country and keyword figures are not presented as current data.

An 11-slide research overview reframed around the experience and distribution questions it raises.

DISCOVERYPLATFORMAUDIENCE

THE DESIGN QUESTION

The original review examines a lock-screen content platform where players can encounter games and other content before opening a conventional app.

Questions explored
01Entry surface→02Player discovery→03Short-session fit→04Content variety→05Return triggers

01 / Distribution experience

The slides cover lock-screen content categories, language personalization and gaming surfaces across supported handsets at the time of the study.

  • The entry point changes how users discover content.
  • Discoverability and friction matter for short-play experiences.
  • The game offer competes with other quick-consumption content.

02 / Gaming catalog and audience

The original deck captures a period-specific overview of Glance gaming, including discovery screens, genre examples and audience snapshots.

  • Look at first-screen clarity rather than assuming an app-store browsing journey.
  • Consider the fit between game genre and session context.
  • Treat historic audience and catalog statistics as examples, not live figures.

03 / Learning takeaway

The useful question is how a platform's entry surface shapes player expectations, not simply how many games its catalog lists.

  • Understand the platform before designing for it.
  • Identify key handoff points from discovery to gameplay.
  • Test whether session length and UI density suit the entry channel.
WHAT THIS STUDY SHOWS

Different discovery contexts create different design constraints—even for the same core game loop.

The Clash of Titans: Board Games vs. Digital Games

Cardboard, controllers and the experiences each medium does best.

PLAYER EXPERIENCEGAME DESIGNOPINION
01

Touch is part of the game

A board game starts before the first move. Someone shuffles the cards. Someone insists the dice are cursed. Someone misplaces an important token. I have always loved that the pieces, the table and the people around it all become part of the experience.

Digital games trade that tangible charm for other superpowers: responsive worlds, animation, sound, narrative and systems that would be nearly impossible to run with a deck of cards. Neither sensation is a lesser version of the other.

02

The people make the rules feel different

Around a board, you can read a raised eyebrow or watch a friend quietly build a winning strategy. The social feedback is immediate. Digital multiplayer creates a different kind of connection: friends can cooperate across cities, strangers can build communities, and an entire world can become a shared playground.

Accessibility also changes with the medium. A board game asks someone to explain its rules and set up the pieces. A digital game can teach through interaction, feedback and tutorials—but it also asks players to navigate controls, devices and interfaces.

03

One ships in a box. The other keeps changing.

Physical games usually need their design to stand on its own once published. Digital games can evolve through patches, new content and LiveOps. That flexibility brings opportunity, but also the responsibility of making each update worth the player's time.

As a designer, I take inspiration from both: the clarity and depth of a strong tabletop rule set, and the feedback and adaptability of a well-crafted digital experience.

ONE LAST THOUGHT

Who wins? The player. The best format is the one that makes the intended experience feel natural.

BLOG · PERSONAL REFLECTION

Let’s Go Sailing: From a Curious Kid to a Game Designer

A little curiosity can take you a long way into game development.

CREATIVE JOURNEYLEARNINGGAME DESIGN
01

The question that starts everything

Have you ever looked at a tiny game on an old phone and wondered, “How does that actually work?” Maybe it was Snakes and Ladders. Maybe it was something else entirely. That small question—who made this, and how?—can open the door to a much bigger world.

What begins as curiosity about a moving character becomes curiosity about rules, feedback, art, sound and the decisions hidden behind every interaction.

02

A game is a ship, not a solo voyage

I like imagining game development as sailing towards an island that does not exist yet. A designer charts a possible route, but a programmer, artist, animator, sound designer and tester all help determine whether the ship gets there.

Sometimes the original map is wrong. A prototype feels confusing. A level is too difficult. A mechanic that sounded brilliant on paper is not fun at all. That is when playtesting, collaboration and iteration become the real compass.

03

There is always another horizon

Learning does not end with a course or a shipped game. New genres, tools and player expectations keep changing the water beneath us. That can be daunting, but it is also what makes this field exciting.

The goal is not simply to finish a game. It is to create moments that someone remembers years later—perhaps with the same curiosity that began the journey.

ONE LAST THOUGHT

Stay curious, keep building, and leave room for the crew to surprise you.

BLOG · INDUSTRY PERSPECTIVE

Women in Games: Pioneering the Future

The pioneers, creators and voices making play more expansive.

WOMEN IN GAMESINDUSTRYCREATIVITY
01

The history is richer than the stereotype

Gaming is often described through a narrow version of its past. But women have been part of making games for decades, even when their work received less attention.

Carol Shaw, known for her work on River Raid, helped establish what early console game design could be. Roberta Williams helped shape the adventure genre through King's Quest and the work of Sierra On-Line. Their contributions are not footnotes; they are part of the medium's foundations.

02

Representation is more than a character model

Games such as The Last of Us Part II and Horizon Zero Dawn show how prominent, complex women protagonists can carry major stories. But representation is not only about who appears on the screen. It is also about who gets to pitch ideas, guide creative decisions and challenge assumptions about players.

More perspectives in the room can mean more possibilities for mechanics, characters, themes and the kinds of experiences teams choose to make.

03

Make opportunity part of the design

Bias, barriers to advancement and workplace harassment remain real challenges in the industry. Mentorship, safer teams, visible role models and genuinely inclusive hiring practices matter just as much as celebrating a successful release.

I want a future where women creating games is not treated as surprising news. Their work should be seen, credited and taken seriously—and the next generation should have room to make things none of us has imagined yet.

ONE LAST THOUGHT

The future of games becomes more interesting when more people have a chance to shape it.
